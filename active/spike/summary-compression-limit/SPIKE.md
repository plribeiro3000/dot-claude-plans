## Investigation question

The engineer's framing, verbatim in intent: "muito resumo é ruim, muito texto é ruim
— você tem que achar o meio do caminho." Too compressed and the reader cannot
reconstruct what happened; too long and it stops being a summary. This spike asks:
what does the evidence say about how much a complex technical explanation can be
compressed before comprehension breaks, and what must be preserved at any compression
level for a reader who lacks session context? The target artifact is the closing
summary of an agent's chat reply — read by a senior engineer who stepped away from a
long technical session and may not know the subsystem being discussed.

## Findings

### Finding 1: Cognitive Load Theory distinguishes load caused by the content itself from load caused by how it is presented

**Evidence:** "Intrinsic cognitive load is the inherent level of difficulty
associated with a specific instructional topic." / "Extraneous cognitive load is
generated by the manner in which information is presented to learners and is under
the control of instructional designers." / "Germane load refers to the working
memory resources that the learner dedicates to managing the intrinsic cognitive load
associated with the essential information for learning."

**Source:** [Wikipedia — Cognitive load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_load)

**Significance:** This gives compression a target: extraneous load (caused by
*presentation* — clutter, redundant framing, unclear structure) is what compression
should remove; intrinsic load (caused by the *content's own* inherent difficulty —
e.g., a genuinely complex causal chain in the change being summarized) is not
reducible by trimming words without losing the content itself. A compression rule
that cuts intrinsic-load content to hit a length target is cutting the wrong layer.

substrings confirmed present in the fetched Wikipedia article text.

### Finding 2: Instructional guidance that helps a novice can hinder an expert — but the literature frames this in terms of domain knowledge, not session-specific context

**Evidence:** "The expertise reversal effect refers to the reversal of the
effectiveness of instructional techniques on learners with differing levels of prior
knowledge." / "Instructional guidance, which may be essential for novices, may have
negative consequences for more experienced learners." / "for higher-knowledge
learners or experts, i.e. learners with more prior knowledge of the task, the reverse
is true, such that reduced guidance often results in better performance than
well-guided instruction."

**Source:** [Wikipedia — Expertise reversal effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expertise_reversal_effect)

**Significance:** This is the closest named concept to the engineer's target reader
(a senior engineer — domain expert — who nonetheless lacks THIS session's specific
context). The effect says added scaffolding that helps a novice can create "redundant
processing" and hinder an expert. But the definition itself is scoped to "levels of
learner knowledge in a domain" and "prior knowledge of the task" — domain-level or
task-level expertise, not session-level familiarity with a specific piece of work
done an hour ago. Whether the effect transfers to the "domain expert, session-context
novice" case this spike asks about is not addressed by the fetched text — see "What
remains uncertain" below.

**Verification block:** URL fetched (2026-07-17) / Verbatim quotes checked (twice,
including an explicit self-check re-fetch) / Quote substrings confirmed present both
times in the fetched Wikipedia article text.

### Finding 3: The coherence principle — exclude extraneous material — as defined on one fetched source

**Evidence:** "People learn better when extraneous words, pictures, and sounds are
excluded rather than included."

**Source:** [JSU Online — Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning](https://www.jsu.edu/online/faculty/cognitive-theory-of-multimedia-learning.html)

**Significance:** This is Mayer's coherence principle, one of the two multimedia
learning principles the spike brief named as worth checking. It gives a rule for
WHAT to cut, phrased as exclusion of extraneous material — consistent with Finding 1
(extraneous load is the reducible target, not intrinsic load).

### Finding 5: The segmenting principle — present complex material in learner-paced chunks — as defined on one fetched source

**Evidence:** "People learn better from a multimedia lesson is presented in
user-paced segments rather than as a continuous unit." (Quoted exactly as it appears
on the source page, including the source's own grammatical construction.)

**Source:** [JSU Online — Cognitive Theory of Multimedia Learning](https://www.jsu.edu/online/faculty/cognitive-theory-of-multimedia-learning.html)

**Significance:** This is the second multimedia-learning principle the spike brief
named. It bears on the trade-off directly: rather than answering "how many sentences
total," it offers an alternative axis — pacing/chunking — as the lever for managing a
genuinely complex explanation, distinct from raw compression. This is a candidate
structural answer to "too compressed vs. too long": chunk what is inherently complex
rather than force it into one dense paragraph or cut it down to nothing.

### Finding 7: What survives compression, named concretely — content on the causal chain connecting a text's opening to its outcome is recalled better than content off that chain, and this effect is stronger than referential (pronoun) connections

**Evidence:** "First, the more causal connections a state has to the rest of the
text (either forward or backward), the better it will be recalled... Second, states
that lie along a causal chain connecting a text's opening to its final outcome are
recalled... better than states not on such a chain. These results support the
conclusion that the causal structure of a text is an important determinant of how it
will be understood and remembered." And, from this paper's own regression analysis:
"both Causal Chain Status and Causal Connections Possible uniquely accounted for
significant proportions of variance, while Referential Connections Possible failed to
account for any significant unique variance." And in the Discussion: "we have found
clear evidence that causal connections contribute to the coherence of a text. The
importance of referential connections remains less clear."

**Source:** Fletcher, C. R., & Bloom, C. P. (1988), "Causal Reasoning in the
Comprehension of Simple Narrative Texts," *Journal of Memory and Language*, 27,
235-244 (replicating and extending Trabasso & van den Broek, 1985) — full text in
`summary-compression-limit_excerpt_1.txt`

**Significance:** This is the sharpest, most concrete answer this spike found to
"what is the irreducible core." It is not about multimedia design or reading speed —
it is a direct, quantitative account of what a reader actually remembers from a
narrative: text elements on the causal chain linking the start of the story to its
outcome, and elements with more causal connections to the rest of the text, are
significantly more memorable; text elements connected only by shared referents
(pronouns, repeated nouns) are not measurably more memorable once causal structure is
accounted for. Applied to a technical closing summary: the causal chain from "what
was done" to "what it caused / what it means for the reader" is the part that must
survive compression; content connected to the narrative only by shared subject
matter, without extending the causal chain, is the more droppable part. This also
complicates the spike brief's hypothesis (§3) that unresolved referents/pronouns are
a primary over-compression failure mode — in this study, once causal structure is
controlled for, referential connectivity did not independently predict recall.

### Finding 8: The one study found that directly tests compression rate against readability found NO consistent pattern — and readability formulas, not human comprehension tests, were the proxy used

**Evidence:** "The texts were summarized using a total of 7 extractive and
abstractive summarization systems with compression rates of 20%, 40%, 60% and 80%. We
analyzed the generated summaries in terms of lexical, syntactic and length-based
features of readability, and concluded that summary complexity depends on the
compression rate, summarization technique and the nature of the summarized corpus."
And, in the conclusion: "Hence no common pattern among the summarization approaches
was identified with respect to the effect of compression rate on readability."

**Source:** Vodolazova, T., & Lloret, E. (2019), "Towards Adaptive Text
Summarization: How Does Compression Rate Affect Summary Readability of L2 Texts?"
RANLP 2019 — full text in `summary-compression-limit_excerpt_2.txt`

**Significance:** This directly answers spike question 1. The one study found that
squarely tests "compression rate vs. readability" across a range of ratios (20-80%)
and multiple systems concludes there is no common, generalizable relationship — the
effect of compression rate on readability depends on which summarization technique is
used and what the source text is like. Separately, and just as important: the 21
"readability" features this study measures (Flesch Reading Ease, Dale-Chall, parse
tree depth, word/sentence length, phrase ratios) are all computational proxies for
text complexity — none of them is a human comprehension test. No human subject in
this study read a summary and was scored on understanding. This confirms the spike
brief's suspicion (§1): the literature that exists on compression ratio measures
either system-output complexity (this study) or summary quality against reference
summaries (a different metric family, ROUGE-type), not human reader comprehension
directly.

### Finding 9: The one numeric compression-rate figure found in the literature (15-30%) measures automatic-summarization system performance, not human comprehension

**Evidence:** "In fact, (C. Y. Lin, 1999) study shows that the best performances of
automatic summarization systems are found with a compression rate of tau = 15 to 30%
of the length of the source document."

**Source:** Yadav, D., Desai, J., & Yadav, A. K., "Automatic Text Summarization
Methods: A Comprehensive Review" — full text in
`summary-compression-limit_excerpt_3.txt`

**Significance:** This is the only place in this spike's research where a specific
numeric compression range appears in the literature at all. It is explicitly a
system-performance figure ("best performances of automatic summarization systems"),
and the surrounding paper's own framing of evaluation (a separate section on
evaluation metrics, not fetched in full here, but referenced by the paper's abstract
and structure) is about scoring machine output against reference summaries — not
about whether a human reader comprehends the result. This is the second, independent
piece of evidence (alongside Finding 8) that the compression-ratio literature that
exists does not answer the human-comprehension question this spike was asked to
investigate; it answers a different, adjacent question (how do you build a good
automatic summarizer, evaluated by overlap with a reference summary).

## Trade-offs surfaced

| Approach | Pros | Cons | Source |
|----------|------|------|--------|
| Cut by extraneous/intrinsic load distinction (remove presentation-caused clutter, keep content-inherent difficulty) | Gives a principled target for WHAT to cut, not just how much (F1, F3, F4) | Requires per-case judgment about which words are "extraneous" vs. part of the causal chain itself — not mechanically checkable | F1, F3, F4 |
| Cut to preserve the causal chain (what happened → what it caused/means), drop content only connected by shared subject matter | Sharpest, most concrete criterion found; directly measured against human recall, not a readability-formula proxy (F7) | The underlying study is about narrative recall of simple stories, not technical explanations specifically — extrapolation to a chat-reply summary is reasoned, not directly tested (F7) | F7 |
| Segment/chunk a complex explanation rather than compress it into one dense paragraph | Named, citable technique distinct from raw shortening; matches the engineer's own "meio do caminho" framing — manage complexity without cutting content (F5, F6) | Segmenting principle research is from multimedia-lesson contexts (narrated animations), not chat-reply summaries; the "learner-paced" mechanism (a click to advance) has no clean analogue in a static closing summary (F5, F6) | F5, F6 |
| Set a numeric length/compression target (e.g., "2-3 sentences", a word count, a percentage) | Simple to state, simple to check | No source found — across two spikes now — establishes a comprehension-safe numeric target; the two sources that DO give a number (F8, F9) both measure something other than human comprehension, and F8 additionally found no consistent effect of compression rate on even the readability-formula proxies it measured | F8, F9, and prior spike's "What remains uncertain" |

## What remains uncertain

- **Whether the expertise reversal effect (Finding 2) transfers from "domain novice
  vs. domain expert" to "domain expert but session-context novice."** The fetched
  literature frames the effect strictly in terms of "levels of learner knowledge in a
  domain" and "prior knowledge of the task" — this spike found no source, verified or
  unverified, that separately studies a reader who is an expert in the general domain
  (software engineering, the specific subsystem's technology) but lacks the
  session-specific context (what was just done, in this specific chat). This is
  reported as a genuine gap, not resolved in either direction.
- **Whether the causal-chain criterion (Finding 7) generalizes from simple narrative
  texts to technical explanation summaries.** The Fletcher & Bloom (1988) study used
  eight short narrative texts (e.g., "The Farmer and the Donkey") with undergraduate
  subjects doing free recall — not technical prose, not the specific reader profile
  this spike targets. The mechanism (causal chain connectivity predicts what is
  remembered) is a general finding about narrative/explanatory text comprehension,
  and the reasoning that it should transfer to "what happened → what it caused" in a
  technical summary is this spike's own extrapolation, not a directly tested claim.
- **Whether removing connectives (the word "because," "so," "therefore") specifically
  — as opposed to removing whole causally-connected content — is itself a distinct
  failure mode.** The one source that would have addressed this directly
  (Comprehension Effects of Connectives Across Texts, Readers, and Coherence
  Relations, tandfonline.com) returned HTTP 403 and could not be fetched; it is
  UNVERIFIED and sustains no claim here (see `summary-compression-limit_sources_1.txt`).
- **No source found — across this spike and the prior one — establishes an
  evidence-backed numeric compression ratio or length target for human reader
  comprehension of a technical summary.** Findings 8 and 9 are the closest this
  research came to a number, and both measure something other than human
  comprehension (readability-formula proxies with no consistent pattern, and
  system-vs-reference-summary quality, respectively).

## Suggested options for main and the engineer

- **Option A — Govern length by content, using the causal-chain test.** The summary
  must preserve the causal chain from "what was done" to "what it caused / what it
  means for the reader"; content connected to the narrative only by shared subject
  matter (background detail, restated context already visible in the tool trace) is
  the droppable part. Sustained by F7 (the sharpest, most concrete criterion found)
  and consistent with F1's extraneous/intrinsic load distinction (F1, F3, F4). Risk:
  this is a judgment call per reply, not a mechanically checkable rule, and the
  underlying research (F7) is on narrative recall, not technical prose specifically
  (see "What remains uncertain").

- **Option B — Explicitly reject any numeric length target, and say so in the rule's
  own text.** Findings 8 and 9 are the only numeric compression figures this spike
  found in the literature, and neither measures human comprehension; combined with
  the prior spike's failure to confirm any "2-3 sentences" folklore, the honest
  position is that no evidence-backed number exists. Sustained by F8, F9, and the
  prior spike's own "What remains uncertain" section. This does not resolve HOW to
  govern length — it only forecloses picking an arbitrary number and presenting it as
  evidence-based.

- **Option C — Adopt segmenting (chunking) as the release valve for a genuinely
  complex chain, instead of forcing a single dense paragraph.** When the causal chain
  (Option A's criterion) has multiple links that cannot be compressed further without
  losing content, split the closing block into a short sequence of prose beats (still
  prose per the prior spike's Finding 8, not bullets) rather than either truncating
  the chain or writing one increasingly long paragraph. Sustained by F5, F6 (the
  segmenting principle) and F1 (intrinsic load is not reducible by cutting). Risk:
  segmenting research is from learner-paced multimedia lessons (a click to advance
  between chunks); a static chat reply has no equivalent pacing control, so the
  chunk-boundary mechanism itself does not directly transfer — only the underlying
  idea (manage complexity by dividing, not by cutting) does.

- **Option D — Combine A and C, explicitly excluding B's numeric-target path.** State
  the rule as: preserve the causal chain (A); if the chain is short, one paragraph
  suffices (matches the prior spike's Finding 15, a one-sentence real-world
  postmortem summary carrying a causal claim); if the chain has multiple necessary
  links, segment into a short sequence of prose beats rather than either cutting
  links or writing one overloaded paragraph (C); never state a numeric sentence/word
  target as if it were evidence-based (B). Sustained by F1, F5, F6, F7, F8, F9, and
  the prior spike's Finding 15.

No option is recommended here — main and the engineer choose. Options A and C are not
mutually exclusive (Option D combines them); B is a constraint on how any option's
language is phrased, not an alternative to A or C.
